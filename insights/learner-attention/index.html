How do we gain and keep learners' attention?

March 2025, by Wenjuan (Joerica) Zou

In a world full of digital noise, attention is one of the most valuable and fragile currencies in learning. If we can't first earn a learner's attention, and then hold it, we lose the chance for meaningful learning altogether. We're competing not just with short attention spans but with real pressures, screen fatigue and other priorities. Designing for attention has to be deliberate.

1. Gaining attention: start strong

Learners engage with material they see as personally meaningful, surprising or emotionally resonant. A question, a story or an unexpected fact gives the brain a reason to care. In a workplace module, I might open with a scenario that mirrors a real challenge, something that triggers a "that's me" moment. The goal isn't to entertain; it's to spark curiosity and signal that this is worth attention.

2. Engaging the elephant

Julie Dirksen's Design for How People Learn uses the metaphor of the rider and the elephant: the rider is our analytical self, the elephant our emotional brain. Most content is written for the rider, but the elephant decides whether to engage. Emotionally relevant scenarios, such as a teammate left out of a meeting or a conflict to resolve, tap into empathy and fairness. When learners feel something, they lean in.

3. Keeping attention: structure matters

Attention fades, especially in self-paced learning, so sustaining it takes active choices: short reflection moments, interactive decision points and micro-assessments that break up content. Rhythm and variation, alternating visuals, audio, text and activities, keep engagement high.

4. Reducing cognitive overload

Cognitive load theory reminds us that too much, or badly structured, content overwhelms working memory and shuts attention down. I use chunking, clear layouts and progressive disclosure, and I anticipate common misconceptions with clarifying examples before confusion takes root.

5. Encouraging self-directed exploration

Autonomy fuels engagement. Optional tracks, real-world cases and branching choices let learners follow their curiosity: novices take a guided route, experienced learners skip ahead or go deeper.

Designing for attention is designing for respect

Attention isn't a default setting; it's earned. When we treat it that way, we invite learners into an experience designed around how they actually think, feel and focus.
